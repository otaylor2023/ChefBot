# Astra pick lab report

Research is in progress. The final policy selection, final development metrics, and final validation result will be filled in after the outstanding experiments finish. Every candidate is preserved in its own `skills/vN` folder; `research_log.md` records the hypotheses, results, and negative findings.

The policy uses Astra to identify the named object and choose calibrated image pixels for a top-surface reference and nearby bare support. Astra chooses a grasp height, any horizontal correction, the finger-closing direction, and the lift distance. For elongated objects, Astra can specify the longitudinal axis and request a perpendicular grasp in the horizontal world plane. This avoids treating perpendicular image directions as perpendicular physical directions. The action space is a top-down parallel-jaw pick primitive.

Code performs depth unprojection using each observation's true camera calibration, converts the selected line into a horizontal direction, builds the flange pose with the 13 cm pad offset, and executes arm-only motion. It chooses the equivalent sign of the same physical finger axis to reduce orientation change. It enforces bounded coordinates, support clearance, reach, and execution time; it does not detect or segment objects, infer grasp success, or use object/category lookup tables. Astra receives fresh pole/base/wrist images and measured gripper width after lifting and decides whether to finish or retry. A retry obtains a new model-selected grasp from new observations. Two attempts are allowed.

Early development established that the example localizes targets but never grasps: 0/2, mean 10.565 seconds. The first complete policy eventually succeeded on 10/10 matched development episodes, but needed retries and averaged 34.506 seconds and 3 calls. Waiting for the arm to catch up improved the matched result to 10/10, 25.253 seconds, and 2 calls. Direct servo targets reduced this to 23.735 seconds on the same ten cases.

The broader 30-category seed-1 test exposed failures hidden by that small set: 28/30 success, mean 27.751 seconds. Corn was missed twice. A cone-shaped object remained held but ended only 2.1 cm higher, despite Astra reporting success. Subsequent development showed three useful corrections: finish wrist orientation before descent; allow extra lift height for objects that rotate or slip downward; and specify long-axis grasps in world-plane geometry. Merely adding a partial-lift action to verification did not cure false-positive success reports in its small isolated test. Relaxing position tolerance from 8 to 10 mm also failed to produce a reliable timing improvement. Longer settling is now reserved for initial orientation rather than every movement.

The first held-out validation submission, v2, achieved reported success 0.889 across 18 episodes, with mean 25.51 seconds, 2 calls, and 23,110 input tokens per episode. Only aggregate output from `./lab val`/`./lab wait` was accessed. Failure causes on validation are unknown. Development trace files were inspected only offline to diagnose timing, contact, and lift behavior; no policy accesses run files or simulator state.

The latency profiles were small and noisy: two matched visual low-effort samples per service tier gave fast 8.434/9.715 seconds, priority 8.262/7.758, and default 13.404/12.587. Medium/fast gave 7.700/8.287 on the same profile prompt. These are insufficient to establish a reliable effort ordering. Policies use low/fast with one request per decision; no request racing or alternate model is used. Explicit short system instructions did not eliminate the substantial request-token overhead visible in the lab's usage accounting.

Known limitations include visibility of the selected surface, imperfect visual height judgment, settling during model latency, pixel/depth error at curved surfaces, and the fixed top-down action space. Objects may rotate during lifting, and a visually clear object can still fail the required displacement. The 15-second target has not been met consistently; even successful versions have occasional episodes exceeding 30 seconds. The final quantitative timing limits will be reported with the final evaluation.

The architecture could be ported to a real robot with the same calibrated RGB-D cameras, RGB wrist camera, arm-state interface, and Cartesian servo. The present evidence is entirely simulated. Deployment would require measured TCP calibration, real-depth robustness, contact/collision safeguards, and validation of the servo and friction assumptions. The current harness relies on accurate depth at model-selected pixels and has no general collision planner.

No lab or documentation files were modified, and no protected category lists, scene files, or validation/test artifacts were inspected. Git commits were unavailable because `.git` is mounted read-only; version directories preserve the changes.
